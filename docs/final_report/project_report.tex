\begin{abstract}
This project integrates official pharmaceutical approval registers from the United States (Drugs@FDA), the European Union (EMA Medicines), and Canada (Health Canada DPD) into a unified dataset across 16 target attributes. We document Phase~I schema mapping and XML translation (52,459 validated records), Phase~II hybrid blocking and entity resolution benchmarking rule-based matchers, four supervised ML classifiers, fine-tuned DistilBERT (0.9215 Macro F1), and a zero-shot LLM (0.9485 Macro F1) with connected-components clustering (2,343 multi-source clusters), and Phase~III PyDI data fusion yielding 49,608 integrated medicine entities across all three registries while raising evaluation accuracy on 31 verified multi-source clusters from 71.29\% strict (75.81\% adaptive) to 84.52\% strict (92.26\% adaptive).
\keywords{Web Data Integration \and Pharmaceutical Data \and Data Translation \and Identity Resolution \and Data Fusion.}
\end{abstract}

% ============================================================
\section{Phase I: Data Selection and Translation}
% ============================================================

This project integrates official pharmaceutical approval registers from the United States (Drugs@FDA~\cite{fda_drugsatfda}), the European Union (EMA Medicines~\cite{ema_medicines}), and Canada (Health Canada DPD~\cite{healthcanada_dpd}) into a unified view across 16 target attributes. Phase~I profiles the source datasets, defines the consolidated XML target schema (\texttt{medicines.xsd}), and produces validated XML exports consumed by Phases~II and~III.

\subsection{Use Case and Input Dataset Profile}

In all three regulatory registers, a single commercial medicine spans multiple product, package, or application rows (such as multiple dosage forms, strengths, or generic ANDA applications), causing raw product tables to outnumber distinct medicines (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). High missingness in the raw extracts is largely structural rather than random, arising from category-specific fields (e.g., veterinary attributes) or regulatory-event dates that apply only to particular approval statuses.

\label{subsec:entity_id}
A medicine is identified by its \texttt{brandName} and \texttt{activeSubstances}. Contrary to initial expectations, \texttt{licenceHolder} cannot serve as an identifying key because identical medicines are frequently marketed by distinct regional subsidiaries or licensing partners across jurisdictions (for instance, \textit{Verkazia} is held by \texttt{HARROW EYE} in the US, \texttt{Santen Oy} in the EU, and \texttt{APOTEX INC} in Canada). Using normalized \texttt{brandName} and sorted \texttt{activeSubstances} keys over the raw extracts yields $1{,}407$ distinct cross-source medicines ($428$ FDA--EMA, $949$ FDA--DPD, $594$ EMA--DPD), which is well above the course minimum of $1{,}000$ overlapping entities (refined to $1{,}372$ unified clusters on the finalized XMLs in Section~\ref{subsec:clustering}). Nevertheless, exact matching misses identical products whose regional International Nonproprietary Name (INN) or US Adopted Name (USAN) spellings, salt modifiers, or formulation suffixes diverge across borders (Table~\ref{tab:difficult_match}).

\subsection{Consolidated Target Schema and Shared Helpers}

The consolidated schema (\texttt{medicines.xsd}) represents one medicine entity per source record with metadata (\texttt{id}, \texttt{source}) and 16 attributes, of which 12 occur in at least two sources (Table~\ref{tab:schema}). Three design conventions ensure downstream uniformity: missing values are omitted rather than emitted as empty tags, repeating attributes (\texttt{activeSubstances}, \texttt{strengths}) use typed wrapper elements, and all dates follow ISO~8601 (YYYY-MM-DD). Controlled vocabularies restrict \texttt{regulatoryStatus} to six standardized lifecycle states (\textit{Authorised, Suspended, Pending, Withdrawn, Application withdrawn, Refused}) and \texttt{productClass} to \textit{Human, Veterinary, Disinfectant}. A shared MapForce library provides ten user-defined functions (Table~\ref{tab:udfs}) backed by lookup XML tables covering 25 regulatory statuses, 227 dosage forms, 147 routes, and 202 strength units.

\subsection{Source Transformations and Untransformable Data}

Table~\ref{tab:field_mappings} summarizes all field correspondences across the three mappers built in Altova MapForce 2027. Each source required targeted cleaning and exclusion rules for information that could not be safely translated:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{EMA Medicines:} Because \texttt{activeSubstances} is mandatory in the target schema, 57 raw rows lacking both \texttt{Active substance} and \texttt{INN} values were excluded via a row filter; another 28 rows with blank primary fields were recovered via INN fallback while preserving multi-word biological names. Human and veterinary ATC columns are merged after stripping provisional asterisks, dropping \textit{Not yet assigned} placeholders, and fixing a known source typo (\texttt{VO4D}$\rightarrow$\texttt{V04D}). Because EMA does not publish strength, dosage form, or route fields in its register export, these three attributes remain structurally absent.
  \item \textbf{Drugs@FDA:} Product records are joined to application metadata on \texttt{ApplNo} and grouped by normalized brand and sorted ingredients, excluding 11 orphan rows and empty-ingredient gases (e.g., \texttt{MEDICAL AIR, USP}). Composite \texttt{Form} strings (such as \texttt{"TABLET;ORAL"}) are split into separate dosage form and route components, and combination strengths (e.g., \texttt{600MG/90MG}) are pre-split before numeric parsing ($99.32\%$ of $60{,}344$ tokens parsed into valid value--unit pairs; $0.68\%$ non-numeric placeholders or concentration ranges such as \texttt{20-300mCi/ML} omitted). Because Drugs@FDA covers only human drugs, \texttt{productClass} maps to \textit{Human}; the \texttt{generic} flag is derived from application types (\texttt{ANDA} vs.\ \texttt{NDA}/\texttt{BLA}) and omitted for mixed entities ($\sim$375 records) to avoid false assignments.
  \item \textbf{Health Canada DPD:} Pre-consolidated from 12 relational tables into one entity-level CSV covering all 16 target attributes, DPD requires fine-grained parsing of semicolon-delimited strength strings into numeric \texttt{(value, unit)} pairs ($99.92\%$ of $100{,}543$ tokens parsed into $100{,}094$ valid pairs across $41{,}574$ medicines; $0.08\%$ placeholders such as \texttt{NIL} or \texttt{TRACE} omitted) and resolution of multi-value historical statuses using a precedence hierarchy ($\text{Authorised} > \text{Suspended} > \cdots > \text{Refused}$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Phase II: Identity Resolution}
% ============================================================

Evaluating the full Cartesian product across the three validated XML sources would require over 472 million pairwise comparisons, motivating stratified benchmark creation, hybrid blocking, matcher benchmarking, and graph-based clustering.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Gold-Standard Benchmark Construction}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

To train matchers, tune blocking recall, and benchmark all paradigms on a shared test set, we built a human-labeled gold-standard dataset of cross-source pairs. Because uniform random sampling across millions of candidate pairs overwhelmingly produces trivial non-matches, we generated annotation batches per source pair in fixed proportions: 50\% suspected hard pairs, 20\% likely ordinary matches (identical normalized brand and full ingredient set), and 30\% random pairs, excluding prior batches across rounds to prevent overlap. To test edge-case disambiguation, the hard-pair quota is distributed equally across four targeted strategies: S1 (identical brand, different ingredient sets), S2 (word-sharing brands, identical ingredients), S3 (word-sharing brands, overlapping ingredient sets), and S4 (high character-trigram TF-IDF cosine brand similarity). Three annotators reviewed 3,498 unique pairs (zero duplicates or conflicting labels), marking a pair as a match if and only if both records share the pharmacological active moiety set and commercial product lineage. Combining all labeled batches, the unified dataset is split 85\%/15\% (seed~42) using joint stratification over \texttt{source\_pair}, \texttt{hard}, and \texttt{match} so that rare cross-border subgroups and hard-to-ordinary ratios stay identically balanced between development (2,973 pairs) and held-out evaluation (525 pairs, Table~\ref{tab:gs_split}).

\subsection{Blocking and Candidate Generation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

We compared PyDI lexical blockers (\texttt{Brand Prefix-4}, \texttt{Brand Trigrams} capped at $1{,}000$ co-occurrences per shingle to prevent fragment explosions, and their union) against zero-shot dense semantic blocking using \texttt{Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B} ($1{,}024$-dimensional encodings of normalized brand and sorted substances, retrieving top-5 cosine neighbors from smaller to larger source) on the $1{,}038$ development positives (Table~\ref{tab:blocking_development}).

Lexical Prefix-4 runs in $0.30\text{ s}$ total across all three source pairs ($0.11\text{ s}$ DPD--EMA, $0.16\text{ s}$ DPD--FDA, $0.03\text{ s}$ EMA--FDA), but misses DPD--EMA matches carrying regional generic prefixes (e.g., \texttt{TEVA-}). Adding trigrams boosts completeness above $98\%$ at the cost of $1.37\text{M}$ candidates. Dense Qwen Top-5 vector search completes in $10.4\text{ s}$ total ($2.5\text{ s}$ EMA$\rightarrow$DPD, $6.8\text{ s}$ FDA$\rightarrow$DPD, $1.1\text{ s}$ EMA$\rightarrow$FDA) and narrows the shortlist to $67{,}355$ candidates with $98.36\%$ pooled development completeness, yet drops to $92.94\%$ ($79/85$) on EMA--FDA held-out test matches, falling below our $97\%$ per-pair target. Generating and saving the embeddings took $496.9\text{ s}$ in original run. Combining both complementary signals as $\mathcal{C}_{\text{final}} = \mathcal{C}_{\text{Qwen top-5}} \cup \mathcal{C}_{\text{Prefix-4}}$ recovers four missed EMA--FDA, one missed DPD--EMA, and one missed DPD--FDA test pairs (Table~\ref{tab:blocking_candidates}), yielding 206,830 candidates ($99.96\%$ reduction ratio) and 98.92\% pooled test completeness.

\subsection{Entity Matching Benchmarks and Error Analysis}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

We evaluated four entity-matching paradigms on the identical 525 held-out test pairs (Table~\ref{tab:all_matchers}), using Macro F1 as primary metric so imbalanced match and non-match classes ($186$ vs.\ $339$ test pairs) contribute equally:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Deterministic \& Rule-Based Matchers:} The naive exact-key baseline matches normalized brands and substance sets, achieving moderate precision but missing over half of all true matches due to salt modifiers and brand suffixes. Three stricter conjunctive rules requiring licence-holder token overlap ($\ge 0.80$ after company-suffix removal, bypassed if missing): Exact, Salt (base moiety sets), and Fuzzy (brand similarity $\ge 0.80$). All three reach near-perfect precision ($0.98$--$1.00$) at low recall because legitimate cross-border licence holders often differ.
  \item \textbf{Supervised ML Classifiers:} Logistic Regression (LR), RBF-SVM, Gradient Boosting (GB), and Random Forest (RF) were tuned via 5-fold stratified cross-validation over six lexical/salt similarity features. Tree ensembles outperform linear and kernel boundaries by 3--4 Macro F1 points, with RF reaching 0.9054 Macro F1 at cutoff 0.50 and 0.9075 Macro F1 at its CV-tuned threshold of 0.48 while fitting in under a second and scoring in milliseconds without API cost. Residual false positives mostly stem from competing generics sharing active substances and similar brand prefixes.
  \item \textbf{Fine-Tuned DistilBERT:} Fine-tuning \texttt{distilbert-base-uncased} over serialized \texttt{brandName}, \texttt{activeSubstances}, and \texttt{licenceHolder} pairs ($2{,}378$ train / $595$ val; $7.54\text{ h}$ CPU training, best checkpoint at epoch 16) yields $0.9193$ Macro F1 at default cutoff $0.50$ and 0.9215 Macro F1 at the validation-tuned threshold $0.17$, outperforming all tabular ML models.
  \item \textbf{Zero-Shot LLM:} Prompting \texttt{gemini-3.8-flash} ($\texttt{temperature}=0.0$) with three strictly blind domain rules over four attributes (no test examples) achieves the highest overall score of 0.9485 Macro F1 ($95.24\%$ accuracy), cutting false negatives by $91.7\%$ compared to the exact baseline.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Error Analysis of Best Matcher.}
Inspecting the 25 test discrepancies ($17$ FP, $8$ FN) of the Zero-Shot LLM reveals four distinct error causes:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Cross-border licensing and acquisitions (14 FP):} The LLM used domain knowledge to link licensed originator brands across different holders (e.g., \textit{Xolair} Novartis/Genentech, \textit{Tekturna} Novartis/LXO, \textit{Doptelet} SOBI/AkaRx), whereas annotators required matching corporate names (plus one \textit{Tevimbra} label-noise pair).
  \item \textbf{Formulation suffix variants (3 FP, 1 FN):} Minor line extensions (\textit{Natpar}/\textit{Natpara}, \textit{Alphagan}/\textit{Alphagan P}, \textit{Afinitor}/\textit{Afinitor Disperz}) were linked by the LLM against negative gold labels, while \textit{Tobi Podhaler} vs.\ \textit{TOBI} was rejected due to distinct delivery devices.
  \item \textbf{Co-brands, prodrugs, and co-formulations (3 FN):} Distinct EU co-marketing names (\textit{Copalia HCT}/\textit{Exforge HCT}), parent-drug vs.\ prodrug pairs (\textit{Emend}/\textit{Ivemend}), and co-formulated hyaluronidase (\textit{Herceptin}/\textit{Herceptin Hylecta}) were missed.
  \item \textbf{Gold-standard label noise (4 FN):} Clinical inspection confirmed the LLM's negative predictions were correct on dual vs.\ triple therapy and three competing generic pairs (\textit{Repaglinide Accord} vs.\ Aurobindo, \textit{Pemetrexed Krka} vs.\ Shilpa, \textit{Tadalafil Lilly} vs.\ Rising).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Production Matching and Connected-Components Clustering}
\label{subsec:clustering}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

To score all $206{,}830$ hybrid-blocker pairs without full-corpus LLM API cost, our production matcher runs three stages:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tiered Rule Scoring:} Combines hand-crafted comparators with LLM normalization heuristics to assign $1.0$ to exact normalized matches ($1{,}938$ pairs), $0.95$ to identical-brand salt variants ($754$), and $0.5 + 0.5 \cdot \text{sim} \in [0.90, 1.0)$ to matching-base-substance pairs with brand similarity $\ge 0.80$ ($2{,}016$).
  \item \textbf{Pruning \& Optional LLM Adjudication:} Prunes $156{,}958$ disjoint-ingredient non-matches upfront and leaves $23{,}446$ low-similarity same-substance pairs unlinked, with optional borderline LLM adjudication for uncertain cases.
  \item \textbf{Greedy 1:1 Filtering:} Sorts the $4{,}708$ positive candidates by score with $(\texttt{id1}, \texttt{id2})$ tie-breaking and filters $1{:}1$ per source pair, removing $1{,}372$ conflicts to yield $3{,}336$ correspondences.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:clustering_profile} contrasts NetworkX connected-components clustering across the exact normalized baseline (\texttt{Exact Base.}, strict brand/substance equality) and our production matcher (\texttt{Cand.\ Match}, the $3{,}336$ filtered hybrid-blocker links). \section{Phase III: Data Fusion}
\label{sec:data_fusion}
% ============================================================

Phase~III integrates attributes across the 2,343 clustered entities and 47,265 singletons using PyDI, benchmarks resolution rules on a verified evaluation set under strict and adaptive comparators, and exports the consolidated XML dataset.

\subsection{Provenance Tracking and Fusion Setup}
All three XSD-validated XML files (52,459 records) and 3,336 greedy 1:1 correspondences are loaded with source metadata, preserving singletons (\texttt{include\_singletons=True}) and record-level dates. Equal source trust weights ($1.0$) support voting rules, whereas ordered jurisdiction priorities ($\text{FDA}>\text{EMA}>\text{DPD}$ and $\text{DPD}>\text{EMA}>\text{FDA}$) are implemented via \texttt{prefer\_higher\_trust} weights ($3, 2, 1$) with fallback when higher-priority values are absent. Text fields are lowercased and whitespace-normalized while preserving \texttt{Decimal} strength amounts and case-sensitive units. Six schema fields remain outside accuracy evaluation: three jurisdiction-specific dates (\texttt{usApprovalDate}, \texttt{euAuthorisationDate}, \texttt{caStatusDate}) with no cross-source conflict, and three policy-dependent fields (\texttt{regulatoryStatus}, \texttt{generic}, \texttt{orphanMedicine}); they retain PyDI's default first-available value in the full XML export.

\subsection{Evaluation Benchmark Construction}
From the 2,247 duplicate-free human clusters, we built a stratified benchmark of 31 unambiguous clusters (seed~42; 12 triplets, 19 pairs; 24 DPD, 27 EMA, 23 FDA records; 310 cells across 10 attributes, deriving \texttt{activeSubstanceCount} from fused \texttt{activeSubstances}), audited against regulatory labels (FDA/DailyMed~\cite{fda_drugsatfda}, EMA EPARs~\cite{ema_medicines}, Health Canada DPD~\cite{healthcanada_dpd}, WHO ATC/DDD~\cite{whocc_atc}) and external labels for 28 groups using $\text{FDA}>\text{EMA}>\text{DPD}$ reference entities (23 FDA, 8 EMA).

\subsection{Density, Consistency, and Adaptive Evaluation Comparators}
Pre-fusion input consistency across the 31 groups averages 77.31\%, ranging from $0.000$ for \texttt{licenceHolder} (distinct regional affiliates) and $0.581$ for \texttt{strengths} to $1.000$ for counts, product classes, and biosimilars (Table~\ref{tab:fusion_results}). Sample attribute density rises to 99.68\% (309/310) after merging because DPD supplies dosage forms, routes, and strengths absent in EMA while DPD and EMA supply ATC codes and biosimilar flags absent in FDA.

Strict tokenized/exact evaluation penalizes harmless formatting, unit, and granularity differences across jurisdictions, so we also evaluate adaptive accuracy using three explicit comparators across all rules:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Licence holder (\texttt{holder\_name\_match}):} Strips punctuation and accepts character similarity $\ge 0.85$ or 1:1 bipartite word matches covering the shorter name ($\ge 0.85$ similarity or $\ge 3$-character prefix), recovering 15 matches (e.g., \textit{pfizer} vs.\ \textit{pfizer inc.}).
  \item \textbf{Strengths (\texttt{strength\_match}):} Uses \texttt{Pint} and \texttt{Decimal} dimensional conversion (e.g., $0.05\text{ g/mL}$ vs.\ $50\text{ mg/mL}$ for \textit{Praxbind}) and $0.5\%$ relative numeric tolerance under 1:1 set matching (e.g., $2.266667$ vs.\ $2.27\text{ mg/mL}$ for \textit{Wegovy}), recovering 3 matches.
  \item \textbf{Dosage form (\texttt{dosage\_form\_match}):} Matches powder families (\textit{powder} vs.\ \textit{powder for solution}) and groups \textit{solution}, \textit{injection}, and \textit{concentrate} into a parenteral liquid family when all source routes are parenteral, recovering 6 matches while keeping mixed-route products such as \textit{Wegovy} distinct.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conflict Resolution Rules and Experimental Results}
We tested built-in PyDI functions (\texttt{longest}, \texttt{shortest}, \texttt{most\_complete}, \texttt{voting}, \texttt{union}, \texttt{intersection}, \texttt{intersection k=2}, and \texttt{FDA/EMA/DPD priority}) alongside two custom domain resolvers across the 9 fusible attributes (Table~\ref{tab:fusion_rule_comparison}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Custom name consensus (\texttt{holder\_consensus}):} Picks the licence holder with highest mean character similarity across sources, reaching only 12.90\% strict and 22.58\% adaptive accuracy because matching regional affiliate strings outvote the reference FDA holder (vs.\ 45.16\% / 93.55\% for FDA priority).
  \item \textbf{Custom unit-normalized union (\texttt{normalized\_strength\_union}):} Converts compatible units with \texttt{Pint} and deduplicates before taking the union, tying plain \texttt{union} at 45.16\% strict/adaptive accuracy (vs.\ 61.29\% / 70.97\% FDA priority) because incompatible dimensions (such as \textit{Praxbind} $50\text{ mg}$ vs.\ $50\text{ mg/mL}$) preserve spurious pairs.
\end{itemize}

Selection maximized adaptive accuracy, then strict accuracy, retaining the simple baseline rule on ties and breaking five remaining ties manually (\texttt{FDA priority} over \texttt{shortest} for \texttt{brandName} and over \texttt{EMA priority} for \texttt{dosageForm}/\texttt{strengths}; \texttt{voting} over \texttt{DPD priority} for \texttt{atcCode}/\texttt{route}). As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:fusion_results}, the selected configuration improves accuracy over the simple baseline from 71.29\% (221/310) to 84.52\% (262/310) strict (+13.23 percentage points) and from 75.81\% (235/310) to 92.26\% (286/310) adaptive (+16.45 percentage points) while preserving 99.68\% density. Because $\text{FDA}>\text{EMA}>\text{DPD}$ yields 23 FDA and 8 EMA reference records, FDA priority gains an expected edge on \texttt{licenceHolder}, \texttt{dosageForm}, and \texttt{strengths}.

\paragraph{Remaining Error Analysis.}
Across 310 cells, 24 adaptive errors remain (9 \texttt{strengths}, 4 \texttt{activeSubstances}, 4 \texttt{dosageForm}, 3 \texttt{atcCode}, 2 \texttt{licenceHolder}, 2 \texttt{route}): missing forms (no source supplies \textit{Vijoice} granules/tablets), unconvertible amounts without volume (\textit{Leqembi} $100\text{ mg}$ vs.\ $100\text{ mg/mL}$), packaging units (\textit{Elelyso} $200\text{ U/vial}$ vs.\ $200\text{ U}$), variant scopes (\textit{Vijoice} packet vs.\ tablet), and voting dropping minority single-source routes (\textit{Wegovy} oral vs.\ subcutaneous). Since all 31 clusters guided rule selection, held-out testing remains future work.

\subsection{Full-Dataset Integrated XML Export}
Running the final rules on all 52,459 XML records and 3,336 links exports 49,608 consolidated entities: 2,343 multi-source clusters (502 \texttt{DPD+EMA+FDA}, 1,388 \texttt{DPD+FDA}, 314 \texttt{DPD+EMA}, 139 \texttt{EMA+FDA}) plus 47,265 singletons (39,429 DPD, 1,692 EMA, 6,144 FDA), passing ID and substance-count checks.

\section{Summary of Overall Results}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

In accordance with the course guidelines, we conclude by answering the three overall evaluation questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Additional Entities Added:} Integrating all 52,459 source records yields 49,608 unique medicine entities (2,343 merged clusters plus 47,265 singletons). Relative to Health Canada DPD (41,636 entities, largest input dataset), integration adds +7,972 net medicine entities (+19.15\%), comprising 139 EMA--FDA clusters, 1,692 EMA singletons, and 6,144 FDA singletons (offset by 3 intra-source DPD records consolidated within the $k > 3$ clusters).
  \item \textbf{Data Density Increase:} Across the 2,343 merged multi-source clusters, cross-source slot filling raises mean attribute density relative to DPD from 0.7364 to 0.9028 (+0.1665) across all 16 schema attributes, from 0.8985 to 0.9916 (+0.0932) across the 12 multi-source attributes, and from 0.9782 to 0.9948 (+0.0166) across the 10 benchmark attributes (reaching 99.68\% on the 31-cluster benchmark). Across all 49,608 groups including singletons, overall mean density shifts slightly from 0.7364 to 0.7319 ($-0.0044$ across 16 attributes; 0.8985 to 0.8856 across 12 multi-source attributes) because the 7,836 added EMA and FDA singletons structurally lack form/route/strengths (EMA) or ATC/biosimilar fields (FDA) and have no DPD partner for slot filling.
  \item \textbf{Overall Dataset Accuracy:} On the 31-cluster (310-cell) verified evaluation benchmark, our selected conflict-resolution rules achieve 84.52\% strict accuracy (262/310) and 92.26\% adaptive accuracy (286/310), improving by +13.23 and +16.45 percentage points over the simple baseline (71.29\% strict, 75.81\% adaptive); no complete-dataset gold standard exists beyond this sample.
\end{enumerate}
